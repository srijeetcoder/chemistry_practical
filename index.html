Experiment 01

Conductometric Titration

Aim: Determination of strength of hydrochloric acid (HCl) using a standard solution of sodium hydroxide (NaOH).

Core Principles

Conductance (G): It is the ease with which electric current flows through a solution. Mathematically, it is the reciprocal of electrical resistance: G = 1/R. Unit is Siemens (S).

Specific Conductance (κ): The conductance of 1 cm3 of electrolyte solution. It relates to conductance via: κ = G × (l/A), where (l/A) is the Cell Constant.

Why does conductance drop, then rise?

1. Before Equivalence Point: When NaOH is added, highly mobile H+ ions (349.8 S cm2 eq-1) are neutralized by OH- to form water, and replaced by slower, heavier Na+ ions (50.1 S cm2 eq-1). Hence, conductance decreases rapidly.

2. After Equivalence Point: All HCl is neutralized. Further addition of NaOH introduces excess highly mobile OH- ions (198 S cm2 eq-1) and Na+ ions. Hence, conductance increases sharply.

Chemical Reaction

H+(aq) + Cl-(aq) + Na+(aq) + OH-(aq) → Na+(aq) + Cl-(aq) + H2O(l)

Neutralization of hydrochloric acid with sodium hydroxide produces soluble sodium chloride and unionized water.

1-Minute Summary

  • Titrand: HCl (in beaker, 25 mL)
  • Titant: Standard NaOH (in burette)
  • Indicator: None! The conductivity meter is the indicator.
  • Endpoint Shape: Sharp V-shape intersection.
  • Common Mistake: Not stirring the solution after each NaOH addition.

Apparatus & Reagents

Apparatus: Conductivity meter, Conductivity cell (dip-type with platinum electrodes), Burette, Pipette, Beakers, Magnetic stirrer.

Reagents: Hydrochloric acid solution (~0.1 N HCl), Standard Sodium Hydroxide solution (0.1 N NaOH), Distilled water.

Step-by-Step Procedure

  1. Calibrate: Calibrate the conductivity meter using a standard 0.1M KCl solution at room temperature.
  2. Prepare Titrand: Pipette out exactly 25 mL of the given HCl solution into a clean 100 mL beaker. Add enough distilled water to submerge the conductivity cell.
  3. Set Up: Dip the conductivity cell into the beaker. Ensure the electrodes are completely covered. Connect the cell to the meter. Record the initial conductance (V = 0 mL).
  4. Titrate: Fill the burette with standard NaOH. Add NaOH in increments of 0.5 mL. Stir gently using the magnetic stirrer after each addition.
  5. Measure: Wait for the reading to stabilize, and record the conductance.
  6. Endpoint Transition: Continue additions until the conductance drops to a minimum, stabilizes, and then rises steadily for at least 5-6 readings.
  7. Plot: Plot Conductance vs. Volume of NaOH added to determine the endpoint.

Precautions

  • Do not touch the platinum black coating inside the cell electrodes.
  • Rinse the cell thoroughly with distilled water before and after use.
  • Ensure there are no trapped air bubbles inside the cell envelope.
  • Stir continuously and wait 30 seconds for stabilization before recording reading.

Conductometric // Q1

Why does conductance decrease first during the titration of HCl against NaOH?

Click to reveal answer →

The highly mobile H+ ions of HCl are gradually neutralized and replaced by slower, heavier Na+ ions from the NaOH solution, causing a drop in overall conductance before the equivalence point.

Experiment 02

pH Metric Titration

Aim: Determination of strength of hydrochloric acid (HCl) using standard sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution pH-metrically.

Core Principles

pH Concept: pH is defined as the negative logarithm (base 10) of the hydrogen ion concentration: pH = -log[H+].

Instrumentation: A pH meter is a high-input-impedance voltmeter that measures the potential difference between a Glass Electrode (active indicator electrode sensitive to H+) and a Calomel Electrode (standard reference electrode). Glass electrode potential is given by: E = E° + 0.0592 pH (at 25°C).

Why does the pH titration curve show an S-shape?

1. Initially: The solution has a high concentration of free H+. Adding NaOH decreases [H+], but because pH is a logarithmic function, the decrease in concentration produces small, slow increments in pH.

2. Near Equivalence Point: When [H+] drops to extremely low levels (e.g. 10-5 M to 10-9 M), adding just a tiny fraction of a drop of base results in a massive log change in H+ activity. This causes pH to jump sharply from ~4 to ~10.

3. After Equivalence Point: Excess OH- is added. The change in pH levels off again because [OH-] dominates and changes slowly in logs.

Chemical Reaction

H+(aq) + OH-(aq) → H2O(l)

The net ionic equation is simply the neutralization of hydronium and hydroxide ions to form unionized water.

1-Minute Summary

  • Indicator: Combined glass-reference electrode.
  • Calibrate with: Standard buffers (pH 4.0, 7.0, and 9.2).
  • Curve shape: Sigmoidal (S-shaped). Inflection point = endpoint.
  • Derivative Graph: Peak of ΔpH/ΔV vs V gives the exact endpoint volume.

Apparatus & Reagents

Apparatus: Digital pH meter, Combined Glass electrode, Burette, Pipette, Beakers, Magnetic stirrer, Wash bottle.

Reagents: Hydrochloric acid (~0.1 N HCl), Standard Sodium Hydroxide (0.1 N NaOH), Buffer tablets (pH 4.0, 7.0, 9.2), Distilled water.

Step-by-Step Procedure

  1. Calibrate: Turn on the pH meter and let it warm up. Standardize it by placing the electrode in pH 7.0 buffer (adjust offset), then pH 4.0 or 9.2 buffer (adjust slope).
  2. Setup Titrand: Pipette exactly 25 mL of the unknown HCl solution into a clean 100 mL beaker. Rinse the combined electrode and place it in the beaker.
  3. Initial Reading: Record the initial pH of the solution before adding any NaOH (V = 0 mL). It should be highly acidic (pH ~1 to 2).
  4. Titrate: Fill the burette with standard NaOH. Add NaOH in 1.0 mL increments initially. Stir gently after each addition and record the pH.
  5. Slow Down Near Endpoint: As pH begins to rise more quickly (above pH 3.5), reduce the increments to 0.1 mL or 0.2 mL to capture the steep curve.
  6. Post-Endpoint: Once pH reaches ~10.5, increase increments back to 1.0 mL. Continue until pH is flat (pH ~12, around 5 mL post-equivalence).
  7. Endpoint Detection: Plot pH vs V, and/or ΔpH/ΔV vs V to locate the equivalence point.

Precautions

  • Never let the glass electrode membrane dry out; store it in 3M KCl or distilled water.
  • Do not allow the glass bulb to collide with the beaker walls or magnetic stir bar.
  • Wash the electrode well with distilled water between transfers.
  • Do not wipe the glass bulb with tissue; blot it gently to avoid static charges.

pH Metric // Q1

What is a glass electrode and what does it measure?

Click to reveal answer →

A glass electrode is an ion-selective membrane electrode sensitive to H+ ions. It measures the change in electrical potential difference across its thin glass bulb relative to H+ concentration in the external solution.

Experiment 03

Argentometric Method (Mohr's Method)

Aim: Determination of chloride ion content in a given water sample using standard silver nitrate (AgNO3) solution.

Core Principles

Precipitation Titration: Chloride ions in water are estimated by titrating against standard silver nitrate (AgNO3). As Ag+ ions are added, they react with Cl- to form an insoluble white precipitate of silver chloride (AgCl).

Indicator: Potassium chromate (K2CrO4) is added. Once all Cl- ions are precipitated, the first excess drop of AgNO3 reacts with chromate ions to form a brick-red precipitate of silver chromate (Ag2CrO4), signalling the endpoint.

Why must the pH be strictly neutral (pH 6.5 to 9.0)?

1. If too acidic (pH < 6.5): Chromate (CrO42-) is converted to hydrogen chromate and dichromate (Cr2O72-) ions:
2CrO42- + 2H+ → Cr2O72- + H2O. Silver dichromate is highly soluble, so the red endpoint precipitate does not form, leading to over-estimation.

2. If too basic (pH > 9.0): Silver ions react with excess hydroxide (OH-) to precipitate silver oxide (Ag2O) or hydroxide (AgOH), which consumes Silver Nitrate prematurely and yields error:
2Ag+ + 2OH- → 2AgOH → Ag2O↓ + H2O.

Chemical Reactions

Ag+(aq) + Cl-(aq) → AgCl(s) ↓ (White precipitate)
2Ag+(aq) + CrO42-(aq) → Ag2CrO4(s) ↓ (Brick-red precipitate)

Note: AgCl (Ksp = 1.8 × 10-10) is less soluble than Ag2CrO4 (Ksp = 1.1 × 10-12 but requires two Ag+, meaning AgCl precipitates first).

1-Minute Summary

  • Titrand: Water sample (containing Cl-)
  • Titrant: Standard AgNO3 (0.02 N or similar)
  • Indicator: 5% Potassium Chromate (K2CrO4)
  • Endpoint Color: Yellow (start) → Brick-red / reddish-brown ppt (end).
  • Water Quality Limit: Chloride content above 250 ppm affects drinking water taste.

Apparatus & Reagents

Apparatus: Burette, Pipette, Conical flask, Measuring cylinder, Wash bottle.

Reagents: Standard Silver Nitrate (AgNO3) solution (usually 0.02 N), Potassium Chromate indicator solution (5%), Distilled water, Sodium hydrogen carbonate (to adjust pH if needed).

Step-by-Step Procedure

  1. Measure Sample: Pipette 20 mL (or 50 mL) of the given water sample into a clean 250 mL conical flask.
  2. Verify pH: Test the pH of the sample. If acidic, add a pinch of solid NaHCO3 to neutralize it and bring the pH to 7-8.
  3. Add Indicator: Add 1 mL of 5% potassium chromate indicator. The solution will turn a clear yellow.
  4. Titrate: Fill the burette with standard AgNO3. Titrate the sample slowly with continuous swirling.
  5. White Ppt Phase: The solution will turn cloudy due to white AgCl precipitate forming. Localized red spots of silver chromate will form and disappear on shaking.
  6. Endpoint Detection: Continue dropwise addition until a faint, permanent reddish-brown/brick-red tinge or precipitate persists.
  7. Record: Note down the burette reading. Repeat titration for concordant values.

Precautions

  • Ensure the pH is neutral. Adjust with NaHCO3 (never use strong acids/bases).
  • Do not add too much indicator; it can mask the endpoint or precipitate early.
  • Silver nitrate stains skin and clothing black upon exposure to light. Handle with care.
  • Perform a blank titration with distilled water if the chloride content is very low.

Chloride Calculation Formula

The concentration of chloride ions in standard parts per million (ppm) is determined as:

Chloride (ppm) = [V × N × 35.45 × 1000] / S

Where:
V = Volume of AgNO3 run down (mL) (Titre value - Blank value)
N = Normality of silver nitrate solution (eq/L)
35.45 = Equivalent weight of chloride ion (g/eq)
1000 = Conversion factor to get milligrams per liter (mg/L = ppm)
S = Volume of water sample taken (mL) (usually 20 mL or 50 mL)

Note: 1 ppm = 1 mg/liter. Normal acceptable drinking water chloride limit is 250 mg/L (ppm). High chloride content causes salty taste and corrodes pipelines.
Argentometry // Q1

Why does silver chloride precipitate before silver chromate starts precipitating?

Click to reveal answer →

Silver chloride (AgCl) has a much lower solubility product ($K_{sp} \approx 1.8 \times 10^{-10}$) than silver chromate ($K_{sp} \approx 1.1 \times 10^{-12}$). Since AgCl requires only one silver ion per formula unit, it reaches its solubility product and precipitates at a much lower Ag+ concentration.

Experiment 04

Determination of Acid Value of Oil

Aim: Determination of acid value of a given oil sample by standardizing potassium hydroxide (KOH) with standard oxalic acid.

Core Principles

Acid Value: The number of milligrams of potassium hydroxide (KOH) required to neutralize the free fatty acids present in 1 gram of oil or fat.

Rancidity: Natural oils and fats are triglycerides (esters of glycerol and fatty acids). During storage, exposure to moisture, light, and heat leads to hydrolysis of triglycerides, releasing free fatty acids. High free fatty acid content causes rancidity (bad smell and taste). The acid value measures this deterioration.

Why standardise KOH using oxalic acid?

Potassium hydroxide (KOH) is a secondary standard because it is highly hygroscopic (absorbs moisture) and reacts with atmospheric CO2. Therefore, it cannot be weighed directly to prepare an exact solution. It must be standardized against a stable primary standard like oxalic acid (H2C2O4 · 2H2O).

Chemical Reactions

1. Standardization of KOH (Oxalic Acid vs KOH):

(COOH)2 + 2KOH → (COOK)2 + 2H2O

2. Neutralization of Free Fatty Acids in Oil (e.g., Oleic Acid):

R-COOH + KOH → R-COOK + H2O

1-Minute Summary

  • Titrand: Oil sample dissolved in alcohol-ether mixture
  • Titrant: Standardized KOH (~0.1 N or 0.01 N)
  • Indicator: Phenolphthalein
  • Solvent: Equal volumes of Ethanol and Diethyl Ether (neutralized to phenolphthalein).
  • Endpoint Color: Colorless to permanent pale pink (lasting 15 seconds).

Apparatus & Reagents

Apparatus: Burette, Pipette, Conical flask, Weighing balance, Beakers, Hot water bath.

Reagents: Oil sample (e.g., coconut, mustard, or olive oil), KOH pellets, Oxalic acid (dihydrate), Neutralized alcohol-ether mixture (1:1 v/v), Phenolphthalein indicator.

Step-by-Step Procedure

Part A: Standardization of KOH

  1. Prepare 100 mL of 0.01 N oxalic acid solution (primary standard).
  2. Pipette 10 mL of oxalic acid into a conical flask and add 2 drops of phenolphthalein.
  3. Titrate with prepared KOH from burette until a permanent pale pink color appears. Note volume (V1).

Part B: Titration of Oil Sample

  1. Weigh exactly 5 grams (or 10g) of the given oil sample into a clean 250 mL conical flask. Record weight (W).
  2. Add 50 mL of neutralized alcohol-ether mixture. Warm gently in a water bath if the oil does not dissolve easily.
  3. Add 2-3 drops of phenolphthalein indicator. Shake well.
  4. Titrate with standardized KOH from the burette under vigorous shaking until a pale pink color persists for 15 seconds. Note volume (V).

Precautions

  • The alcohol-ether solvent mixture must be neutralized before dissolving the oil. Otherwise, the acidic properties of the solvent will result in high acid values.
  • Vigorous shaking during titration is essential because oil is immiscible with water and reactions occur at interface.
  • Do not expose the pink endpoint color to open air too long; atmospheric CO2 neutralizes the excess base and fades the color.

Oil Acid Titration

Solution Color
Clear/Colorless
KOH Added: 0.0 mL Endpoint ~ 4.5 mL

Acid Value Calculations

Step 1: Normality of KOH (S1)

S1 = (10 × 0.01) / V1

Where V1 is the volume of KOH used to neutralize 10 mL of 0.01 N oxalic acid.

Step 2: Acid Value of Oil

Acid Value = (56.1 × V × S1) / W

Where:
56.1 = Molecular weight of KOH (g/mol). Represents that 1 mL of 1 N KOH contains 56.1 mg of KOH.
V = Volume of standard KOH consumed for oil titration (mL)
S1 = Strength (Normality) of KOH determined in Step 1 (N)
W = Weight of the oil sample taken (grams)

Acid Value // Q1

Why is a mixture of alcohol and ether used to dissolve the oil sample?

Click to reveal answer →

Oils are triglycerides (esters) which are completely insoluble in water. A 1:1 mixture of ethyl alcohol and diethyl ether dissolves the oil, bringing both the fatty acids and the KOH titrant into a single homogeneous phase for rapid neutralization.
